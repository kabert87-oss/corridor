Issue 030·14 September 2026·Continental·Framework: Corridor Index

African pension funds are buying the continent's hotels. Five foreign brands keep 80 percent of the control.

African Capital, Foreign Control. Africa's hotel-development pipeline stands at a record 123,846 rooms across 675 projects, and five foreign groups, Marriott, Hilton, Accor, IHG and Radisson, hold roughly 80 percent of it. What has changed is who pays: increasingly the money is African, with pension funds in South Africa, Zambia and Tanzania taking lead ownership of the buildings. But owning the freehold is not the same as controlling the asset. Under the standard management contract, the foreign brand takes a fee on revenue whether or not the hotel profits, a second fee on the profit, mandatory spending on the brand's own standards and systems, and a booking charge on every guest, across a term of twenty to thirty years the owner cannot cheaply escape. This issue reads the gap through the Corridor Index: the continent is winning the ownership and losing the control, and in hospitality the control is where the value lives.

Somewhere in Livingstone, Zambia, the Radisson Blu overlooking the Mosi-oa-Tunya falls is owned by the National Pension Scheme Authority, the fund that holds the retirement savings of Zambian workers. In South Africa, the Municipal Employees Pension Fund owns hotel assets at OR Tambo International and is developing more. This is the newest and least-remarked feature of Africa's hotel boom: the capital building the continent's branded hotels is, increasingly, African, and specifically the pooled savings of African workers, channelled through pension funds moving from government bonds into real assets.1 On the question this publication has asked all month, who owns the asset, the answer in hospitality is turning, encouragingly, toward the continent itself. And yet the value is not staying with the owner, because in the hotel business ownership of the building and control of the business are two different things, sold under two different contracts, and Africa is winning one while losing the other.

The Corridor Index framework reads a tourism economy by tracing where the value is captured. Earlier issues followed it out through the booking platform, the airport concession and the currency peg. This issue follows it through the most physical asset of all, the hotel itself, and finds that even here, even when an African institution holds the freehold, the structure of the international management contract routes a durable share of the returns to the foreign brand, and hands it operational control the owner cannot easily reclaim. The building is African. The business inside it is not.

Who is building Africa's hotels

The scale first. Africa's hotel-development pipeline reached a record 123,846 rooms across 675 hotels and resorts in the 2026 industry survey, up more than 18 percent on the previous year, on the back of the continent's position as the world's fastest-growing inbound tourism region, with 81 million international arrivals in 2025.2 This growth is genuine and it is welcome. But it flows through a strikingly narrow channel of operators. Five global groups, Marriott, Hilton, Accor, IHG and Radisson, account for about 80 percent of all pipeline hotels and rooms, with Marriott alone holding some 31,782 rooms, Hilton close to 19,600 and Accor above 18,000.2 The continent's branded hospitality future is being built, overwhelmingly, under five foreign flags. For lenders and developers this concentration is a feature, not a flaw: an internationally recognised brand reduces perceived risk, reassures financiers, and plugs a property into a global reservation and loyalty system. The reliance is rational at the level of the individual deal. Its aggregate effect on where the value lands is the subject of this issue.

The two contracts, and why the difference matters

A branded hotel typically sits on top of two separate legal relationships, and conflating them is the error that makes the ownership numbers misleading. The first is ownership: who holds the freehold, put up the capital, and carries the risk of the building. Increasingly, in Africa, that is a local investor or pension fund. The second is operation: who runs the hotel day to day, sets its standards, hires and directs its staff, and controls its systems. That is the brand, under a management contract, and it is where control resides. There is also a lighter variant, the franchise, in which a local operator runs the hotel under the brand's flag and pays for the name and the reservation system while keeping operational control; the franchise leaks less, and the choice between the two models is precisely the choice this issue is about. Under the dominant management-contract model, the owner who financed the entire asset does not run it, cannot unilaterally change how it is run, and pays the operator a stack of fees for the privilege, on a contract measured in decades.3

The stack of fees, itemised

The fee structure is documented in hotel-industry surveys and in the filings of the listed companies that own such hotels, and it has four layers worth separating because each captures value differently. The first is the base management fee, typically 2 to 4 percent of the hotel's gross revenue, paid off the top line regardless of whether the hotel makes a profit; as the industry's own analysts note, a fee on revenue rather than profit does not necessarily reward the operator for controlling costs.3 The second is the incentive fee, commonly 8 to 10 percent of gross operating profit and in some documented agreements reaching 20 to 25 percent of operating profits, paid on the bottom line, so the brand is compensated on both the revenue and the profit of a building it did not finance.4 The third is the layer the outside analyst most often misses: mandatory brand-standard spending. The management agreement obliges the owner to furnish, fit out and refurbish the property to the brand's specifications, frequently through the brand's own required suppliers and periodic renovation cycles, and in some contracts to lease furniture and equipment on terms the operator sets, converting the owner's capital budget into a channel the brand directs.5 The fourth is the recurring charge: fees for the central reservation system, the loyalty programme, marketing and technology, levied per booking or as a percentage, so that the distribution the owner is already paying a management fee to access carries its own additional toll.5

Ownership without control

Put the fee stack together with the contract's duration and its termination terms, and the shape of the thing becomes clear. Management agreements commonly run twenty to thirty years, with renewals often at the operator's option, and early termination by the owner triggers compensation calculated on the fees the brand would have earned over the unexpired term, so an owner who wants to reclaim control of the business must pay years of fees not yet earned to buy its way out.4 The result is an asset the owner cannot fully direct. The pension fund holds the freehold and the risk; the brand sets the standards, controls the operating decisions, directs a share of the capital spending, and collects on revenue, profit and bookings alike, for a generation. This is the mechanism worth naming, because a commission figure or an ownership statistic each obscures it on its own. Call it the ownership-control gap: the separation, by long contract, of the party that owns and finances a tourism asset from the party that controls it and captures its recurring value, such that local ownership of the building coexists with foreign capture of the business. The continent can win every argument about who owns African hotels and still lose the one that determines who profits from them.

The honest limit of the argument

The management contract is not a swindle, and the case against it can be pushed past what the evidence supports. The international brands deliver genuine and hard-to-replicate value: a global reservation and loyalty system that fills rooms, operating expertise that a first-time owner lacks, brand recognition that commands higher rates and occupancies, and, decisively, the financeability without which many of these hotels would not be built at all, since lenders extend capital against a recognised flag that they would withhold from an independent. A pension fund is often a passive institutional investor that neither wants nor is equipped to run a hotel, and for it the management contract is a rational division of labour, not a defeat; the fees buy competence and demand it could not supply itself. And the very rise of African institutional ownership is a real gain over the era when the building, too, was foreign. The Corridor's claim is therefore the specific one: the management-contract model captures a large and durable share of hospitality value for the foreign operator, it concentrates that capture among a handful of brands controlling 80 percent of the pipeline, and an African owner or state that treats the flag as the only financeable option, without weighing franchise alternatives, regional brands, or the negotiation of control and procurement terms, is accepting a distribution of value it has not tested against the alternatives. Owning the building is progress. Mistaking it for owning the business is the error.

Three tests over the next year

The first test is contractual literacy: whether African institutional owners, and the pension-fund boards now deploying workers' savings into hotels, negotiate the terms that actually govern value capture, the base against the incentive fee, the mandatory-procurement clauses, the length and termination cost, or accept standard templates written for the operator's benefit, since the difference between a well-negotiated and a standard management contract is a measurable share of the asset's lifetime returns.3 The second is the growth of the alternatives: whether African regional hotel brands and independent operators, and the franchise model that leaves operational control with the owner, gain enough scale and lender recognition to give owners a genuine choice beyond the five global flags, because a choice of one is not a choice.2 The third is where the pension money ends up over time: whether the institutions channelling African retirement savings into these buildings come to measure their returns net of the full fee stack and against the control they conceded, and to ask whether the citizens whose savings built the hotel are capturing the value the hotel generates, or merely holding its risk. Africa is buying its hotels. Whether it comes to own the business inside them is the question the next decade will answer.
